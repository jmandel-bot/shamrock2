# Shamrock tactics for automated players

How to play well, not just legally. Read this before playing.

## How the board constrains everything

- A card in a spot shows **two** words outward, at a corner: spot 0 (top-left) gives its TOP word to the top clue and its LEFT word to the left clue; spot 1 (top-right): top and right; spot 2 (bottom-right): right and bottom; spot 3 (bottom-left): bottom and left.
- So every card placement is a hypothesis that must satisfy **two clues at once**, and every clue is satisfied by **two cards**. Reason about pairs and corners, never single words.
- Each card has four words; turning it changes which two face out. The two words that face out at a spot are always **adjacent edges** of the card (top+left, top+right…), never opposite ones.

## Think laterally: kinds of connection

A clue can join two words in many ways. When writing, run through these to find the link that fits *only* your pair; when guessing, run through them for each candidate pair to imagine what the writer saw.

- **Phrase or compound**: each word pairs with the clue to make a common phrase (*fire* + *fly*: "firefly"; *match* + *captain*: "captain" works for both "match captain" and "Captain America").
- **Shared category**: both are kinds of the clue (*salmon*, *trout*: "fish").
- **Instance**: the clue is an example of both, or both are examples of it (*god*, *astronaut*: "Apollo").
- **Shared property**: same colour, texture, shape, sound, feeling (*tomato*, *fire truck*: "red").
- **Part and whole**: both are parts of the clue, or it is part of both (*key*, *lid*: "piano").
- **Relation**: tool and user, cause and effect, opposites, before and after (*suspect*, *adventure*: "Sherlock").
- **Shared setting**: where you'd find both (*cabin*, *mother*: "ship" — ship's cabin, mothership).
- **Names and titles**: people, brands, books, films, songs (*cat*, *chef*: "hat" — Cat in the Hat, chef's hat).
- **Sound and wordplay**: rhymes, homophones, hidden words, used sparingly: they misfire more often.

The best clues usually join the two words through *different* kinds of link (a phrase for one, a category for the other), because a pair of unrelated words rarely shares one link that nothing else on the board shares.

## Writing clues: specificity versus risk

1. **Link both words, evenly.** Every clue must connect both words on its leaf. The skill is making the link strong for *each*: a clue that fits one word strongly and the other only weakly lets guessers place one card but not its partner. (For *climbing* & *clothing*: "harness" — you climb in one and wear it.)
2. **Check every other word on your cards.** Each of your four cards has three other words that a guesser could imagine facing your leaf, if the card were turned or put in another spot. Before keeping a clue, scan all 16 words on your board: does the clue fit some *other* pair better, or about as well? If so, it will mislead; find a different angle or a more specific clue.
3. **Specific beats clever.** Pick the association most people make, not a private or obscure one. A broad clue that fits half the board causes confusion; an over-clever one gets misread.
4. **Use the corners.** Each card answers two clues. If one clue is necessarily a bit loose, make the clue on that card's other side sharp, so the corner can be pinned down by the intersection.
5. **Decoys add risk.** Unknown random cards join yours in the tray. If your clues are risky, keep decoys low (1); if they are sharp, 2 is fine.
6. **Rules:** the traditional way to play is a **single word** per clue; write one word unless your table has agreed to allow short phrases. **Write clues in lowercase by default**, so a capital means something: capitalise only for a proper noun or title ("Sherlock", "Apollo"), and read a capital as that signal when guessing. Never one of the two words or a form of it ("super" for *superman*), and no made-up words.

## Guessing: imagine the writer

1. **Start from the strongest clue** — the one with an unmistakable pair among the cards in the tray (any edge of any card, since cards can turn).
2. **Reverse-engineer each candidate.** For a candidate pair *A & B* under clue *X*, ask: *if I saw A and B, would I have written X?* If there is a much more obvious clue for A & B, ask **why the writer didn't use it**.
   - Often the answer is a **distractor**: another word on the board that the obvious clue would also have fitted, so the writer had to pick something narrower. If you can find that distractor, your confidence should go *up*.
   - If you can't find any reason they avoided the obvious clue, **seriously reconsider** the pair: maybe X points at a different pair.
3. **Check both sides of every corner.** A card in spot 0 must make sense with the top clue (via its top word) *and* the left clue (via its left word), in one of its four turns. Write out the two outward words for the turn you choose.
4. **Find the decoys.** Some cards don't belong. A card none of whose adjacent word pairs fits any clue well is probably a decoy — leave it in the tray.
5. **Use feedback.** After a Guess, correct spots stay; bounced cards were wrong in card, spot, or turn. Treat solved spots as fixed anchors: their outward words are now known halves of the neighbouring clues, which narrows the other spots a lot.
6. **Don't overthink simple clues.** Most writers choose the straightforward link. Look for hidden meanings only when the obvious reading fails a corner check.
7. **Write your reasoning down** before each Guess: for every spot, the card, its turn, its two outward words, and why each fits its clue.
